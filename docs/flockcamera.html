Data & Power: Ethics of Flock Safety Use

Ethical Considerations of Recent Flock Safety Operational Changes
Author

William Albrecht

Published

November 23, 2026

Introduction

Flock Safety, a surveillance company founded in 2017, has raised nearly $1 billion in venture capital funding (Marlow, 2026; University of Washington Center for Human Rights [UWCHR], 2025). It creates and sells solar-powered automated license plate readers (ALPRs); more than 120,000 cameras are in service nationwide (Marlow, 2026; UWCHR, 2025). The cameras collect visual data on every single car that passes by, and AI interprets the images and stores descriptions of each car in Flock’s centralized database (UWCHR, 2025). Flock users can search by license plate, car make and color, or smaller details like bumper stickers and roof racks (UWCHR, 2025). The software can also analyze movement patterns and list vehicles that have visited a location of interest (UWCHR, 2025). Each customer controls its own network of cameras and chooses whether to share its data with agencies in the same state, within a set radius, nationwide, with specific agencies, or not at all (UWCHR, 2025). Many businesses and neighborhood associations choose to share their camera data with police, including Home Depot and Lowe’s (UWCHR, 2025).

Every search requires the user to enter a reason (UWCHR, 2025). Agencies also get monthly audits, displaying who searched, the license plate, the reason, and the case number, but the audits show only what was searched, not what the search found (UWCHR, 2025). In May 2025, 404 Media revealed that police had searched Flock’s network on behalf of ICE. These searches came to light only because the officers entered “ICE” or “illegal immigration” as the reason for the search (404 Media, as cited in UWCHR, 2025). That same month, a user from the Johnson County Sheriff’s Office in Texas searched the Prosser, Washington, police department’s Flock network. The reason they entered was “had an abortion, search for female” (UWCHR, 2025). Once an agency has access to a network, the only thing standing between an officer and a driver’s location history is the reason that the officer types into the search field. UWCHR researchers call this reliance on officer-entered reasons “inherently problematic” (UWCHR, 2025). If an officer wants to search for someone who had an abortion or on behalf of ICE, they can enter a vague reason and the search still goes through; the only difference that a vague reason makes is that no one can tell afterward what the search was for. The data science component is Flock’s AI, which turns images of every passing car into a searchable database of vehicle locations and movement patterns; the ethical dilemma is that the same system police use to solve crimes also tracks every driver, and its safeguards depend on officers’ honesty.

Permission Structure

A permission structure is the set of rules for who may access the data and for what purpose. The question is whether Flock’s rules hold up. Flock has two facets to its permission structure: the network owner deciding who can access the data they capture, and the reason field (why the data was requested). UWCHR’s review of Washington law enforcement agencies’ Flock audits found that, from at least May 9 to August 24, 2025, local networks were subject to “thousands of searches by ‘US Border Patrol [Federal]’” (UWCHR, 2025). These show up in the audits of at least ten Washington police departments that “did not explicitly authorize Border Patrol searches of their network data,” including Prosser and Renton (UWCHR, 2025). Users appear to be able to search more networks than were shared with them: Renton’s sharing report lists 91 organizations, but its audit shows searches across “up to 6511 networks” (UWCHR, 2025). It is unclear whether Border Patrol had “back door” access or whether departments activated “National Lookup,” which is turned on “by checking a single box in system settings”; the records “did not reveal whether that box had been checked” (UWCHR, 2025). Either way, UWCHR (2025) notes that “it may be challenging for system administrators themselves to understand the implications of the settings they have activated.” Flock claimed that its new “Proactive Search Term Tool” would block any “impermissible” searches, but reasons like “investigation” or even “hehehe” were still approved by the system (Marlow, 2026). Flock then switched to a drop-down list of reasons that police could pick from, but they could easily be dishonest in their selection (Marlow, 2026). Both sources show the same failure, as the vague reason “investigation” that was approved in Marlow’s example also appears in Washington, where Border Patrol users gave “exceedingly vague reasons, such as ‘investigation’ or ‘targeting’” (UWCHR, 2025). Flock’s permission structure was not reliably followed: the networks of at least ten departments that did not explicitly authorize Border Patrol were searched by Border Patrol anyway, the records do not show whether any of them agreed to it, and the reason field approved vague reasons while the later drop-down menu let police choose a reason “whether it was accurate or not” (Marlow, 2026; UWCHR, 2025).

Unintended Use

Data is used in unintended ways when information collected for one purpose is put to another use that the people in it never expected or agreed to. Flock’s own announcement shows what its system is meant to be used for, offering the example that “City A could allow City B to search its cameras for a stolen vehicle or violent crime while blocking searches related to immigration enforcement” (Marlow, 2026). Washington law also forbids using local data for immigration enforcement, as the Keep Washington Working law “bars law enforcement agencies across the state from dedicating local resources for purposes of civil immigration enforcement” (UWCHR, 2025). However, Washington data still reached federal immigration agencies through “side door” searches, which are “searches by authorized network users on behalf of other agencies without access to the network,” often carried out “as an informal favor or act of assistance to a federal colleague” (UWCHR, 2025). In June 2025, agents from Homeland Security Investigations and other agencies sent emails to law enforcement listservs “asking other officers on the list to run a search on their behalf,” and officers from “multiple Western Washington jurisdictions” ran the searches (DivestSPD, as cited in UWCHR, 2025). On May 23, 2025, a user from the Yakima County Sheriff’s Office ran two searches with the reason “ICE,” which UWCHR says was “presumably referring to U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement,” and the searches “were applied to 89 networks” (UWCHR, 2025). UWCHR (2025) concludes that Washington officers’ “active assistance of ICE in this case almost certainly constitutes a violation of Keep Washington Working.” The data has also been turned toward personal use: Marlow (2026), citing The Washington Post, describes “a growing pattern of police officers turning Flock into a personal stalking tool,” noting that “dozens of officers have recently been arrested, fired, or otherwise disciplined for misusing Flock for personal reasons.” Therefore, by Flock’s own example and by Washington law, Flock’s data is being used in unintended ways: a system that Flock presents as a tool for solving crimes like vehicle theft and violent crime ends up serving federal immigration enforcement and officers’ personal motives.

Identifiability

Data is identifiable when it can be traced back to a specific person, and it is only free of ethical concerns if it has been sufficiently anonymized or is too old to reveal anything meaningful about that person’s life. Flock’s data is neither anonymized nor reliably deleted. Because users can search Flock’s database by license plate, its records can be tied to specific vehicles (UWCHR, 2025). Washington’s Department of Licensing database “makes it possible to identify a vehicle’s registered owner by searching a full or partial license plate number,” while ALPRs “make it possible to pinpoint that vehicle’s location as it moves through the world” (UWCHR, 2025). Immigration enforcement already uses license plates this way, as UWCHR researchers found that license plate data is “frequently cited in Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) records as a tool used in the identification and location of targets” (UWCHR, 2025). Over time, these records reveal more information than what streets a car travels on. The Electronic Frontier Foundation warns that “by matching your car to a particular time, date, and location, and then building a database of that information over time, law enforcement can learn where you work and live, what doctor you go to, which religious services you attend, and who your friends are” (Electronic Frontier Foundation, as cited in UWCHR, 2025). Marlow (2026) adds that Flock photographs “every vehicle passing their cameras,” making it possible to “track those vehicles and apply AI to uncover their occupants’ private patterns of life.” Deleting location data quickly would limit how much it can reveal, but Flock’s retention rules do not guarantee that the data is deleted quickly. Flock announced it would reduce its standard retention period from one month to one week, but its new “Evidence Mode” preserves data “when a search is tied to a case number,” and “agencies can still set retention to whatever their state or local policy requires” (Marlow, 2026). Marlow (2026) warns that even if Evidence Mode only keeps the results of a search, “it would still retain significant amounts of location data on persons and vehicles who law enforcement do not suspect have engaged in any wrongdoing.” Therefore, Flock’s data is identifiable, not anonymized, and can be kept long enough to remain harmful, so anonymity is not guaranteed for any driver who passes one of its cameras.

Variables, Accuracy, and Missing Data

Data can only support reliable conclusions if its variables are recorded accurately and important observations are not missing. This applies to Flock’s monthly audits, which Flock’s CEO emphasized as a way to ensure that users “are aware of the data-sharing choices they have enabled on their network” (UWCHR, 2025). Each audit records variables including “the identity of the user making the search,” the number of networks and devices searched, the license plate number, and “the ‘reason’ asserted for the search” (UWCHR, 2025). The reason is the variable meant to show why a search was made, but it is self-reported by the officer, and UWCHR (2025) notes that Flock’s safeguards “rely on the assumption that law enforcement officers self-report their search reasons in good faith.” As a result, researchers cannot verify it. When a SeaTac officer entered the reason “bp” on January 23, 2025, UWCHR suspected it meant Border Patrol, but the city later said it referred to the Bellevue Police. UWCHR (2025) concludes that it “has no way to verify what ‘bp’ referred to in this particular instance.” The audits also leave out what each search found, so for the Yakima “ICE” searches, UWCHR (2025) states that “it is not known whether the information yielded in these searches resulted in actual immigration enforcement activities.” Similarly, Organization Audits “do not offer any information about the identity of the networks searched by a given organization, only the number of networks and devices searched,” so it is impossible to tell whether an agency’s searches reached public or private networks (UWCHR, 2025). In some cases, the audits are missing entirely. UWCHR requested Flock records from 48 Washington law enforcement agencies but had received records from only 35 (UWCHR, 2025). Sedro-Woolley denied the request for its audits, stating, “We have not run reports for either an organization audit or a network audit in Flock, therefore no records exist” (UWCHR, 2025). Arlington, Everett, Kent, Medina, and Tukwila also refused to release their Network Audits, arguing that the reports “had not been reviewed by their agencies and do not constitute public records” (UWCHR, 2025). Marlow (2026) adds that because the number of officers misusing Flock is unknown, “we cannot conclude if Flock and its auditing tools are catching 95 percent of violators or 5 percent,” and that the effectiveness of Flock’s “Audit Assistance” tool “has never been independently evaluated.” Therefore, Flock’s audit data cannot show whether its system is being used legitimately: the variable meant to explain each search is self-reported and unverifiable, and the records that would reveal misuse, from search results to entire audits, are often missing.

Conclusion

The problems with Flock’s permissions, unintended uses, identifiable data, and missing records show who gains from Flock’s system and who pays for it. Flock benefits most directly, as Marlow (2026) argues that its “nearly $1 billion in venture capitalist funding has locked it into an operational model that seeks to trade our privacy for massive profits.” Law enforcement also benefits, since ALPRs “may be a potent tool for law enforcement” (UWCHR, 2025). Federal agencies gained access to Washington data as well, through thousands of Border Patrol searches and favors from local officers (UWCHR, 2025). Yet Flock places responsibility for how its data is shared and used on its customers, as its CEO stated, “It is a local decision. Not my decision, and not Flock’s decision” (UWCHR, 2025). Every driver who passes a Flock camera has their car recorded, and UWCHR (2025) warns that ALPR misuse “can undermine the reproductive and migrant rights which Washington state has committed itself to defending.” For immigrants, this exposure comes at a time when, as UWCHR (2025) notes, “many of our neighbors are being subjected to raids by federal immigration authorities, which may result in fates as dire as their being forcibly disappeared.” For people seeking abortions, the risk is criminal charges: after a Texas officer searched Flock’s networks nationwide to find “a woman who had self-administered an abortion,” law enforcement “had considered bringing criminal charges against her” (404 Media, as cited in UWCHR, 2025). Flock’s ethical violations served profit, surveillance, and power. Flock profits by collecting and sharing data on every car that passes its cameras. Law enforcement has used that data for surveillance, including searches on behalf of immigration agencies and a search for a woman who had an abortion. Officers and agencies decide who can search a driver’s data and for what reason, and the driver has no say in either decision. Marlow (2026) argues that until Flock “demonstrates they can develop a reliable, workable system to prevent improper searches,” its promise of local control “provides nothing more than a false sense of security.”
